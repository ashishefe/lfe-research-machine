How the model works

Two things make AI models better: more training compute, and better methods, which we call software. Tejas assumes software supplies two-thirds of progress. If training compute keeps its pace, doubling overall progress needs software progress to run 2.5 times as fast.

Better methods come from a research lab. The lab needs researchers, human or AI, and computing power for experiments, and he weights them equally. Add researchers without adding experiment compute and each extra one adds less. That is why 2.5 times the pace of new methods takes 2.5 × 2.5 = 6.25 times the labour.

Research labour here means human researchers plus AI doing research work, measured in researcher-equivalents. A 6.25 does not mean 6.25 times the staff. Swapping human researchers for AI raises this number. It adds no experiment compute.

Where this comes from

Tejas called his post a scrappy summary, his loose impression of tweets by Ryan Greenblatt, Ajeya Cotra and others. He says falling research productivity could push the figure much higher. This page builds his model so you can change its assumptions. The starting software share, Cobb-Douglas form, labour weight, A and E are his, and the 2x is METR's. The compute split, the time path and the other settings are ours.

What the number depends on

These cautions are ours.

  • The model treats AI researchers as free of compute. In practice they run on compute too, which would compete with experiments and training.
  • Tejas supposes the two-thirds split and the equal weights. Change them and the answer moves.
  • METR's benchmark index gave 1.9x, with a 95% interval of 1.5x to 2.8x. Its 2x judgement also draws on other evidence, and it says Anthropic redacted most of what it found most informative.
  • Much of METR's evidence measures well-specified tasks, and METR calls its estimate uncertain.
  • The index and effective compute are different things. Reading one as the other assumes they move together.
  • METR guesses that perhaps 80% of the acceleration in October 2026 came from AI. Tejas's model gives AI all of it.
  • Tejas says falling research productivity could raise the labour figure much further. The 'Ideas 20% harder' case shows one example.

Inside the model

This is the same model as The machine, with its parts named. Two equations do the work: one makes new methods, the other makes better models. You can change anything here and the machine changes too. Hover over or tap any symbol to see what it means, or open In plain English under a card.

About this model

Two sectors, in growth rates. Time is in years; growth is in doublings per year. Every ratio compares the AI world with the baseline path at the same date.

The research sector extends the Romer and Jones setup by adding experiment compute. The ideas-getting-harder extension draws on Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb, Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find? (American Economic Review, 2020).

T0, W, β, φ and L0, K0 defaults are illustrative. We did not estimate them.

Numbers in substitutions are rounded; ≈ marks approximate arithmetic.

Final sector · Training run

Effective compute = C × S g0 = s × T0 c0 = (1 − s) × T0

g0 ≈ 0.67 × 3 ≈ 2 c0 = (1 − 0.67) × 3 ≈ 1 doublings/year.

In plain English

A model gets better in two ways: more training compute, or better methods. The two multiply, so doubling either one doubles effective compute. In growth rates the multiplying becomes adding: growth in effective compute equals growth in C plus growth in S. That lets us split baseline growth: s to software, 1 − s to training. Tejas supposes two-thirds and one-third. No research labour appears in this equation. Research labour comes in one step earlier, in the research lab. Methods are non-rival: each run uses all of S.

Research sector · Research lab

g = g0 × A × F(L, E)λ G = g/g0 = A × F(L, E)λ F(L, E) = La × E1 − a

With a = 0.5 and λ = 1, this is Tejas's equation: G = A × √L × √E.

F = 6.250.5 × 11 − 0.5 ≈ 2.5 G ≈ 1 × 2.51 ≈ 2.5 Cobb-Douglas a ≈ 0.5 F(1,1) = 1.

In plain English

This equation makes new methods. Its output is a pace: how fast S grows. Its inputs are research labour, human or AI, and experiment compute. With equal weights and λ = 1, neither input can do the work alone: four times the labour only doubles the pace. A scales the whole result. If ideas get harder to find, A falls and the same lab finds less. Cobb-Douglas builds in one fixed answer to how far more research labour can stand in for more experiments, so no symbol for it appears here. Choose CES below to set it yourself.

Compute link · Compute pool

E = k × φ1/φ0 Cr = k × (1 − φ1)/(1 − φ0) c = c0 + log2(Cr)/W

E ≈ 1 × 0.5/0.5 ≈ 1 Cr ≈ 1 × (1 − 0.5)/(1 − 0.5) ≈ 1 c ≈ 1 + log2(1)/1 ≈ 1.

In plain English

A lab has one pool of compute and two uses for it: experiments and training. φ₀ and φ₁ are the shares that go to experiments before and after the change. If the pool stays the same, more for experiments means less for training. If the pool doubles and the split stays the same, both double. Experiments get a change at once. A change in training takes W years to show in full. The experiment dial on The machine holds Cr fixed.

Reading the index · AI capability

Xe = (G × g0 + c)/T0 Xout = 1 + (Xe − 1)/(b × h)

Xe = (2.5 × 2 + 1)/3 ≈ 2 Xout = 1 + (2 − 1)/(1 × 1) ≈ 2.

In plain English

The model works in effective compute, but METR reports a benchmark index. This card turns one into the other. With h and b both at 1, the two speedups are the same. h says how much of the speedup AI caused. b says how the index speedup relates to the effective compute speedup. Only b × h enters. Lowering h means more of the reported speedup comes from outside AI, so the same inputs report a larger speedup. With h below 1, the labour figure covers only the part attributed to AI. The target X enters through Xe = 1 + b × h × (X − 1).

Holding the pace

G1 = (Xe × T0 − c)/g0 G2 = (Xe × T0 − c0)/g0 D(t) = max(0, G1 − 1) × g0 × min(t, W) + max(0, G2 − 1) × g0 × max(0, t − W) A(t) = A × 2−β × D(t)

At year 5: G1 ≈ 2.5 G2 ≈ 2.5 D(5) ≈ 15 A(5) ≈ 0.0743 L(5) = 1,131.37x.

In plain English

Going faster uses up the easy ideas faster. Unless β is 0, A keeps falling while the fast pace lasts, so the labour needed climbs. A cut in training compute can make it drop once, at year W. Read the shape of the line. Its size depends on β and on g₀, which comes from T₀. Both are our illustrative numbers.

From the target to labour

  1. Target X ≈ 2 → Xe = 1 + 1 × 1 × (2 − 1) ≈ 2.
    Why this step

    Start from the target speedup. Adjust it for how much AI caused and for how the index relates to effective compute.

  2. G = (Xe × T0 − c)/g0 = (2 × 3 − 1)/2 ≈ 2.5.
    Why this step

    Take away what training compute supplies. Software must supply the rest.

  3. Tg = (G/A)1/λ = (2.5/1)1/1 ≈ 2.5. Tg is the required value of F.
    Why this step

    Divide by research productivity, then undo the power λ. This gives what the lab's research labour and experiment compute must produce.

  4. L = (Tg/E1 − a)1/a = (2.5/11 − 0.5)1/0.5. L ≈ 6.25x.
    Why this step

    Undo the production function to find the labour that produces it.
