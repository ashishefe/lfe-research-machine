Is there an intelligence explosion?

If better AI could make AI research go faster, and that faster research made AI better still, very capable AI could arrive much sooner than most people, companies and governments are planning for. That is why researchers are arguing about whether it has started.

The takeaway

Anthropic's AI has been improving faster since late 2025. That could be one big step up, a faster but steady climb, or a climb that gets faster with every step. Only the last is an explosion, and the public numbers cannot yet tell the three apart.

Three shapes a speed-up can take

Each tick marks a doubling of the length of task AI can do. The number above it is how many months that doubling took.

What is being measured

The pages track how long a task AI can do. METR, an independent group that tests AI models, measures this as the length of task, in the time a skilled person would take, that a model completes half the time. In early 2025 the best model managed software tasks that take a person about an hour. By late 2025 it was about five hours. That is not how long AI can work alone, and the uncertainty is wide: roughly a factor of two either way, METR says, and wider for the newest models.

MEASURED Claude 3.7 Sonnet, about one hour (METR, March 2025). Claude Opus 4.5, about 4 hours 49 minutes, 95% range 1 hr 49 min to 20 hr 25 min (METR, January 2026). The capability index on these pages is a different scale; Garicano and Imas convert it to task length at about five index points per doubling.

Where things stand

Last updated 15 November 2026.

1. AI research at Anthropic has sped up. After October 2025, Anthropic's AI models improved about 1.9 times as fast as before, on METR's measure.

CLAIMED METR, 5 November 2026, on data Anthropic shared. Its estimate is 1.9 times, with a 95% range of 1.5 to 2.8.

2. Nobody yet knows how much of that came from more money and staff, and how much from AI tools. Progress can speed up because more goes in, because more comes out of the same input, or both, and the two can feed each other: better AI can attract the money that buys more research. Anthropic spent far more, and each dollar bought less progress than before. The question is whether progress beat what the extra spending alone would predict. The model-based study discussed here, by Garicano and Imas, finds that it did, in part: more inputs explain some of the speedup, and the rest looks like a one-time boost, though they say that estimate is uncertain. A one-time boost is not an explosion, which needs feedback strong enough to keep raising the growth rate as AI improves. Telling these apart is the hard part, and it is what these pages work through.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas, 13 November 2026. On their figures, progress per dollar fell about 69%.

3. In AI research, the easy ideas seem to be getting used up. On Garicano and Imas's model, each step forward costs more than the last, as studies have found in many other fields. That rests on their assumptions, and how fast it is happening is uncertain.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas for AI. Bloom and others for other fields.

4. The public data show no clear sign yet of AI feeding its own research in a way that speeds up as AI improves. That loop is what an explosion would need. AI is already helping with research, and METR, using evidence it could not publish, judges that AI accounts for much of the faster pace. The public figures can neither confirm nor rule that out.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas, from their model. METR on AI's share.

5. It cannot be ruled out. Daniel Kokotajlo, an author of the AI 2027 scenario, wrote that if METR is right, an explosion now looks likely in 2027. In Garicano and Imas's broadest test of measurement error, about one in eleven re-runs of their model meet its condition for an explosion. That shows how much the answer depends on noisy scores.

CLAIMED Kokotajlo, 10 November 2026, and the AI Futures post he links. Garicano and Imas, online appendix, section F8. It is not a probability, independent of their model, that an explosion has begun.

The one number that decides it

In the simplified model used here, with spending held fixed, one number sets whether growth speeds up or slows down as AI improves. It sets how strongly AI's gains feed back into research against how quickly new ideas get harder to find. If the feedback is weaker, each step costs more than the last and growth fades unless spending keeps rising. If it is stronger, each step makes the next easier and growth runs away. The feedback here comes from AI doing more research. The spending dial is set from outside the model; it does not model AI earning the money for more research. The selected estimates sit below the line. Their positions depend on the model specification and how progress is measured. They do not pin down where today’s AI sits.

The record

Each entry: who and when, what they claim, the kind of evidence, and how it fits with the rest. Newest first.

Tom Davidson: the speed-up may not be mostly money Tom Davidson, senior research fellow at Forethought. Thread on X, 15 November 2026, with a reply from Alex Imas the same day.

What they claim. Garicano and Imas conclude that the faster progress at Anthropic is mostly explained by faster growth in research spending, so we are no closer to an explosion. Davidson argues this rests on how much research Anthropic is assumed to have had before 2024. Using the spending in Anthropic's filings, he finds cumulative spending grew about 3.3 times a year before October 2025 and 4.4 times after, while progress went from about 15 to 29 points a year. So each doubling of cumulative spending bought more progress than before: about 9 points, then about 14. On that reading, returns to research rose, which is a step towards the threshold. In his figures, φ rises from about 0.45 to 0.65.

Kind of evidence. A re-analysis of Garicano and Imas's model with different starting figures. No new data.

Questions. How much of the speedup is money, and how much is AI? Are ideas getting harder to find in AI?

Their caveats. Davidson says he may have missed something. Imas replied that the right starting figure is not what Anthropic spent but the cumulative research behind everything it began with, including what it inherited from the field, and that this cannot be read from the filings. Their fits put it at $1.0 to $2.1 billion. Near $2 billion, returns look the same before and after October 2025; nearer $1 billion, either returns rose or productivity jumped once. Imas agreed the paper should have been clearer about this.

How it fits. It turns the question on this site into one about a single assumption: how much past research Anthropic was building on in early 2024. It bears directly on Fiddling with ASI, whose settings come from Garicano and Imas's fits; Davidson points out that their separate before-and-after fits let that starting figure reset between periods. The spending puzzle explains why spending figures alone cannot separate rising returns from a one-time jump.

How sure. Claimed. A disagreement about one input to the model, with both sides' figures shown.

Dwarkesh Patel: the case that AI research will soon automate itself Dwarkesh Patel, podcaster and writer. ‘The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development’, essay with an appendix, 13 November 2026.

What they claim. His median year for automated researchers to match the best human AI researchers is 2028, assuming labs do not intentionally slow down; he gives a 25% chance this happens after 2032. He expects AI at least as good as top human experts in every field by 2030, possibly sooner. His reasons: AI already speeds up AI research; in fields where answers can be checked, such as maths, AI went from weak to beyond the best humans within a few years; and most of AI research can be checked too, through training results, test scores and efficiency. Once research is automated, he sets AI improving its own research against ideas getting harder to find. If AI wins, he expects a ‘software-only singularity’; if difficulty wins, a ‘spark’ of three to six years of progress in one year, then a slowdown until more computing is built. He calls the two equally likely.

Kind of evidence. An argument with forecasts, drawing on figures published by METR and the labs. No new data.

Questions. Is AI starting to do its own research? What would an explosion need, and what would we see first?

Their caveats. He gives a 25% chance that full automation comes after 2032, mainly because AI may still be unable to learn on the job. He grants that experiments, data and choosing what to measure are real bottlenecks that make the fastest takeoff less likely. His probabilities are personal judgements.

How it fits. The strongest case for an explosion on this page, and the closest to its framing: stories 4 and 5 in Fiddling with ASI show the shape of his two outcomes, though story 4's spark is smaller than his. It answers Chalmers directly, arguing that research judgement can partly be checked and trained; Toby Ord, whom he quotes, doubts that AI can yet ask the deep questions. It supplies the half our research machine leaves out: the machine works out how much research labour holding the pace would need, and Dwarkesh argues AI researchers will supply it. Like Garicano and Imas, he notes that labs must roughly quadruple their research computing each year just to hold the pace. Where Garicano and Imas find no clear sign yet of AI feeding its own research in the public data, his evidence is what the labs report from inside.

How sure. Claimed. A forecast built on an argument. We checked four figures he cites against their sources: METR's guess that about 80% of Anthropic's October 2026 speedup came from AI, which METR calls subjective; Anthropic's report that Claude ‘leads’ 26% of its AI research work as of August 2026, up from under 1% in February, where ‘leads’ means completing most of a task end to end while a human supervises; OpenAI's report that its 90th-percentile researcher uses more than $7,000 of AI tokens a day; and OpenAI's report that its agents found a proof for the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem about 88 hours after the first agents started, followed by about 17 more hours for formalization and verification in Lean. OpenAI researchers directed groups of agents, moved resources between problems and shared intermediate findings; the Navier–Stokes work used about 130 billion output tokens. We did not check his other figures.

Luis Garicano and Alex Imas: what more data shows Luis Garicano and Alex Imas, economists. ‘Are we approaching an intelligence explosion?’, Silicon Continent, 13 November 2026, with an online appendix.

What they claim. Using Anthropic's filings, METR's chart and their own staff estimates: progress per dollar fell about 69% and per technical employee about 38%. Fitting a standard model of research to sixteen model scores, more inputs explain part of the speedup (at most about half, in their most generous version) and the rest looks like a one-time jump in research effectiveness. On their model, ideas are getting harder to find, at a similar rate before and after October 2025; that rests on their assumptions, and the estimates are imprecise. No clear sign yet of AI supplying research input that grows with capability.

Kind of evidence. Measurement and a fitted model.

Questions. How much of the speedup is money, and how much is AI? Are ideas getting harder to find? Is AI starting to do its own research?

Their caveats. Staff numbers are their estimates from published shares; the final score falls inside their effort-only forecast's margin of error; the most generous jump estimate is not statistically clear; with a one-year research delay the per-dollar fall shrinks from about 69% to about 40% (from 64% to 10% in a January-to-September comparison); in their broadest test of measurement error, about one in eleven re-runs of their model meet its explosion condition, which is not a probability, independent of their model, that one has begun; results are model comparisons, not measured effects of AI tools. Their appendix says they worked with Gemini Carbon, Fable 5.2 and Astra.

How it fits. The fullest analysis of the spending puzzle we have found. Its broad reading sits near Krier's, with a one-time boost that the per-dollar chart could not show. It does not contradict METR's guess about AI's share, which concerns a later date.

How sure. Claimed. Arithmetic checked against the filings; the model was not refitted by us.

David Chalmers: the ‘factor F’ question David Chalmers, philosopher. Post on X, 11 November 2026, with replies to 12 November.

What they claim. Several researchers read METR's 2x speedup as support for an intelligence explosion around 2027. Chalmers asks how that reasoning works. He describes the main rival view, which he calls the "factor F hypothesis", and says he does not know whether it is true: there is at least one capacity F (he suggests creativity, research vision or planning) that an AI must have to reach superhuman research, and that is hard enough to build that leading systems will not have it soon. The 2x speedup, he says, reflects gains in other capacities G, such as coding and experiments, where the speedup may be larger, perhaps 10x. Under the F hypothesis, G can explode on its own and give an "intelligence subexplosion" below general intelligence. He does not see why the speedup makes the F hypothesis much less likely. Routes from 2x to an explosion in 2027 need "huge premises", such as extrapolating ever-faster speedups. He adds that he is worried about near-term superintelligence all the same.

Kind of evidence. An argument about what the evidence shows. No new data.

Questions. Is AI starting to do its own research? What would an explosion need?

How it fits. It gives a name to a gap the economics leaves open. Garicano and Imas find a one-time jump in research effectiveness "consistent with" coding agents. Chalmers's picture would predict gains of that kind if they come in G and not F. He also allows that G could improve itself fast, a "subexplosion" below general intelligence. The research machine cannot show this directly: its σ setting is about research labour against computing, not about one research skill against another. Chalmers's F is a bottleneck of the second kind. Replies push back. Daniel Kokotajlo: many candidate Fs have been proposed before and overtaken quickly. One reply suggests strong G could let AI build F itself, and Chalmers grants the point. Peli Grietzer offers a reading Chalmers endorses: METR's report is evidence for "if general ASI is coming soon then general ASI is coming very soon".

How sure. Claimed. An argument about what the evidence shows; no new data.
