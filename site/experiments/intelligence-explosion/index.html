Is there an intelligence explosion?

An intelligence explosion would mean AI improving AI research so strongly that the growth rate of AI's abilities keeps rising, instead of settling at a new, faster pace. Researchers, economists and philosophers now disagree about whether one has begun. This page gathers the work worth reading on that question, explains it in plain words, and says how it fits together. We update it as new work appears.

OUR GUESS "Worth reading" is our judgement.

Two ways research could go

Think of research as a curve. Along the bottom is the total effort put in so far: money, staff and computers. Up the side is progress on some measure of AI's abilities. In the fields economists have studied, the curve bends over: the easy ideas get found first, so each step costs more effort than the last. An explosion would need the curve to bend the other way, with AI's improvements feeding back into research strongly enough to outrun the rising difficulty. The feedback could come from AI doing more of the research, from AI earning money that pays for more research, or from both. How bent the curve looks also depends on how progress is measured.

Where things stand

Last updated 14 November 2026.

1. AI research at Anthropic has sped up. After October 2025, Anthropic's AI models improved about 1.9 times as fast as before, on METR's measure.

CLAIMED METR, 5 November 2026, on data Anthropic shared. Its estimate is 1.9 times, with a 95% range of 1.5 to 2.8.

2. Nobody yet knows how much of that came from more money and staff, and how much from AI tools. Progress can speed up because more goes in, because more comes out of the same input, or both, and the two can feed each other: better AI can attract the money that buys more research. Anthropic spent far more, and each dollar bought less progress than before. The question is whether progress beat what the extra spending alone would predict. The model-based study discussed here, by Garicano and Imas, finds that it did, in part: more inputs explain some of the speedup, and the rest looks like a one-time boost, though they say that estimate is uncertain. A one-time boost is not an explosion, which needs the feedback to keep strengthening. Telling these apart is the hard part, and it is what these pages work through.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas, 13 November 2026. On their figures, progress per dollar fell about 69%.

3. In AI research, the easy ideas seem to be getting used up. On Garicano and Imas's model, each step forward costs more than the last, as studies have found in many other fields. That rests on their assumptions, and how fast it is happening is uncertain.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas for AI. Bloom and others for other fields.

4. The public data show no clear sign yet of AI feeding its own research in a way that speeds up as AI improves. That loop is what an explosion would need. AI is already helping with research, and METR, using evidence it could not publish, judges that AI accounts for much of the faster pace. The public figures can neither confirm nor rule that out.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas, from their model. METR on AI's share.

5. It cannot be ruled out. Daniel Kokotajlo, an author of the AI 2027 scenario, wrote that if METR is right, an explosion now looks likely in 2027. In Garicano and Imas's broadest test of measurement error, about one in eleven re-runs of their model meet its condition for an explosion. That shows how much the answer depends on noisy scores. It is not a probability, independent of their model, that an explosion has begun.

CLAIMED Kokotajlo, 10 November 2026, and the AI Futures post he links. Garicano and Imas, online appendix, section F8.

The questions inside the question

Is AI research speeding up?

Yes, at Anthropic, by about 1.9 times on one measure, with a wide range. → METR.

How much of the speedup is money, and how much is AI?

Unsettled. The answer depends on what you assume would have happened anyway. → Krier and Wiebe; Garicano and Imas; our spending puzzle.

Are ideas getting harder to find in AI?

Probably, on Garicano and Imas's estimates. How fast, and whether the rate is changing, is uncertain. → Garicano and Imas.

Is AI starting to do its own research?

AI already helps with parts of research. The public data show no clear sign yet of the self-feeding kind. David Chalmers asks a related question: the gains so far may be in the easier parts of research, such as coding and running experiments, and could grow fast there without reaching harder parts such as research judgement. → Garicano and Imas; Chalmers.

What would an explosion need, and what would we see first?

AI that gets better at the hard parts of research, not only the easy ones, which is Chalmers's question. And feedback, through better research tools or through money, strong enough that returns to research effort stop falling and start to rise, which is the condition in Garicano and Imas's model. → Chalmers; Garicano and Imas.

What would reduce the uncertainty?

Model-by-model scores with their uncertainty, the research bill broken into its parts, how long spending takes to show up, and comparisons fixed in advance. → our spending puzzle, section 7.

The record

Each entry: who and when, what they claim, the kind of evidence, and how it fits with the rest. Newest first.

Dwarkesh Patel: the case that AI research will soon automate itself Dwarkesh Patel, podcaster and writer. ‘The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development’, essay with an appendix, 14 November 2026.

What they claim. AI as good as the best human AI researchers is likely by 2028 (his middle guess, with a 25% chance it comes after 2032), and AI at least as good as top human experts in every field by 2030, possibly sooner. His reasons: AI already speeds up AI research; in fields where answers can be checked, such as maths, AI went from weak to beyond the best humans within a few years; and most of AI research can be checked too, through training results, test scores and efficiency. Once research is automated, he sets AI improving its own research against ideas getting harder to find. If AI wins, he expects a ‘software-only singularity’; if difficulty wins, a ‘spark’ of three to six years of progress in one year, then a slowdown until more computing is built. He calls the two equally likely.

Kind of evidence. An argument with forecasts, drawing on figures published by METR and the labs. No new data.

Questions. Is AI starting to do its own research? What would an explosion need, and what would we see first?

Their caveats. He gives a 25% chance that full automation comes after 2032, mainly because AI may still be unable to learn on the job. He grants that experiments, data and choosing what to measure are real bottlenecks that make the fastest takeoff less likely. His probabilities are personal judgements.

How it fits. The strongest case for an explosion on this page, and the closest to its framing: his two outcomes are the two curves in the figure above. It answers Chalmers directly, arguing that research judgement can partly be checked and trained; Toby Ord, whom he quotes, doubts that AI can yet ask the deep questions. It supplies the half our research machine leaves out: the machine works out how much research labour holding the pace would need, and Dwarkesh argues AI researchers will supply it. Like Garicano and Imas, he notes that labs must roughly quadruple their research computing each year just to hold the pace. Where Garicano and Imas find no clear sign yet of AI feeding its own research in the public data, his evidence is what the labs report from inside.

How sure. Claimed. A forecast built on an argument. We checked four figures he cites against their sources: METR's guess that about 80% of Anthropic's October 2026 speedup came from AI, which METR calls subjective; Anthropic's report that Claude ‘leads’ 26% of its AI research work as of August 2026, up from under 1% in February, where ‘leads’ means completing most of a task end to end while a human supervises; OpenAI's report that its 90th-percentile researcher uses more than $7,000 of AI tokens a day; and OpenAI's report that its agents found a proof for the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem in about 88 hours, with a machine-checked version. We did not check his other figures.

Luis Garicano and Alex Imas: what more data shows Luis Garicano and Alex Imas, economists. ‘Are we approaching an intelligence explosion?’, Silicon Continent, 13 November 2026, with an online appendix.

What they claim. Using Anthropic's filings, METR's chart and their own staff estimates: progress per dollar fell about 69% and per technical employee about 38%. Fitting a standard model of research to sixteen model scores, more inputs explain part of the speedup (at most about half, in their most generous version) and the rest looks like a one-time jump in research effectiveness. On their model, ideas are getting harder to find, at a similar rate before and after October 2025; that rests on their assumptions, and the estimates are imprecise. No clear sign yet of AI supplying research input that grows with capability.

Kind of evidence. Measurement and a fitted model.

Questions. How much of the speedup is money, and how much is AI? Are ideas getting harder to find? Is AI starting to do its own research?

Their caveats. Staff numbers are their estimates from published shares; the final score falls inside their effort-only forecast's margin of error; the most generous jump estimate is not statistically clear; with a one-year research delay the per-dollar fall shrinks from about 69% to about 40% (from 64% to 10% in a January-to-September comparison); in their broadest test of measurement error, about one in eleven re-runs of their model meet its explosion condition, which is not a probability, independent of their model, that one has begun; results are model comparisons, not measured effects of AI tools. Their appendix says they worked with Gemini Carbon, Fable 5.2 and Astra.

How it fits. The fullest analysis of the spending puzzle we have found. Its broad reading sits near Krier's, with a one-time boost that the per-dollar chart could not show. It does not contradict METR's guess about AI's share, which concerns a later date.

How sure. Claimed. Arithmetic checked against the filings; the model was not refitted by us.

David Chalmers: the ‘factor F’ question David Chalmers, philosopher. Post on X, 11 November 2026, with replies to 12 November.

What they claim. Several researchers read METR's 2x speedup as support for an intelligence explosion around 2027. Chalmers asks how that reasoning works. He describes the main rival view, which he calls the "factor F hypothesis", and says he does not know whether it is true: there is at least one capacity F (he suggests creativity, research vision or planning) that an AI must have to reach superhuman research, and that is hard enough to build that leading systems will not have it soon. The 2x speedup, he says, reflects gains in other capacities G, such as coding and experiments, where the speedup may be larger, perhaps 10x. Under the F hypothesis, G can explode on its own and give an "intelligence subexplosion" below general intelligence. He does not see why the speedup makes the F hypothesis much less likely. Routes from 2x to an explosion in 2027 need "huge premises", such as extrapolating ever-faster speedups. He adds that he is worried about near-term superintelligence all the same.

Kind of evidence. An argument about what the evidence shows. No new data.

Questions. Is AI starting to do its own research? What would an explosion need?

How it fits. It gives a name to a gap the economics leaves open. Garicano and Imas find a one-time jump in research effectiveness "consistent with" coding agents. Chalmers's picture would predict gains of that kind if they come in G and not F. He also allows that G could improve itself fast, a "subexplosion" below general intelligence. The research machine cannot show this directly: its σ setting is about research labour against computing, not about one research skill against another. Chalmers's F is a bottleneck of the second kind. Replies push back. Daniel Kokotajlo: many candidate Fs have been proposed before and overtaken quickly. One reply suggests strong G could let AI build F itself, and Chalmers grants the point. Peli Grietzer offers a reading Chalmers endorses: METR's report is evidence for "if general ASI is coming soon then general ASI is coming very soon".

How sure. Claimed. An argument about what the evidence shows; no new data.
