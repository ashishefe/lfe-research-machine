1. The puzzle

Fiddling with ASI

ASI stands for artificial superintelligence: AI better than the best humans at nearly everything.

↻One road there runs through a loop. AI gets better at AI research, so research goes faster, so AI gets better again.

←Against that loop pulls a stubborn fact: in every field studied so far, each new idea is harder to find than the last.

ESTABLISHED The model is Charles Jones's model of research (1995), with the AI loop added by Philippe Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones (2019). Luis Garicano and Alex Imas fitted it to Anthropic's numbers in November 2026.

Which force wins? On the model below, it comes down to one number. Move it and the future changes from a slowdown to steady growth to an explosion. Nobody yet knows which side we are on. This page lets you find the line yourself.

2. The loop

3. The model in words

growth of AI's abilities=(how effective research is)δ×(research effort)(how much more you get from more effort)λ×(abilities so far)((how much past progress helps the next step) − 1)φ

research effort=(spending)×(abilities so far)(how much AI adds to research)ψ

What each one means:

  • How effective research is (δ). How much progress a unit of effort buys today. A one-time boost, such as better tools arriving, raises it once.
  • How much more you get from more effort (λ). At 1, double the effort and you double the new ideas. Below 1, part of the extra effort is wasted, for example when researchers duplicate each other's work.
  • How much past progress helps the next step (φ). Below 1, each advance makes the next harder: ideas get harder to find. At 1, progress keeps its pace on its own. Above 1, each advance makes the next easier.
  • How much AI adds to research (ψ). At 0, better AI supplies no extra research effort through this term; AI can still raise productivity once (that is the boost). For example, at ψ = 1, each doubling of AI’s ability doubles the research effort supplied through this term.

4. The one number that decides it

Put equation (2) into equation (1). Research effort is partly made of abilities, so abilities now appear twice: once through the effort AI supplies, and once through how hard the next idea is. Together they leave abilities raised to the power λψ + φ − 1 in the growth of abilities.

growth of AI's abilities = δ × spendingλ × (abilities so far)λψ + φ − 1

In the simplified model used here, with spending held fixed, whether growth speeds up or slows down as AI improves depends on one number, the loop's strength, λψ + φ.

In common sense. Each time AI doubles its abilities, two things happen to the speed of progress.

  • A push. Better AI does more of the research, so more effort goes in. That push is λψ: how much more research the better AI supplies, times how much that extra research is worth.
  • A pull. The next idea is harder to find. That pull is 1 − φ. In equation (1) it appears as the power φ − 1, a negative number; 1 − φ is the same thing with the sign flipped, so the pull is a positive size you can set against the push.

If the push is bigger than the pull, each doubling makes the next one come sooner. That is λψ + φ above 1. If the pull wins, each doubling takes longer than the last, unless someone keeps spending more.

ESTABLISHED This is the condition in Aghion, Jones and Jones, and the one Garicano and Imas test.

Why above 1 would be new

In research, for as long as anyone has measured, the pull has won.

  • Chips. Keeping Moore's law going now takes more than 18 times as many researchers as in the early 1970s. Better chips did help design the next chips, yet the number of researchers needed kept rising.
  • Every field studied. The same economists found research productivity falling in farming, medicine and across firms: steady progress needed ever more researchers.
  • The one loop that ran hot. For most of human history, more people meant more ideas, more ideas fed more people, and population grew faster the larger it was. That loop broke when richer families chose fewer children: the push stopped feeding back.

What makes AI different is that the push could come from the thing being improved. A better chip did not become a better engineer. A better AI might become a better AI researcher, and copies of it can be made in minutes, not raised and trained over twenty years. That is why AI is new territory for this equation.

CLAIMED Chips and other fields: Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb, 2020. Population: Michael Kremer, 1993.

Below 1, exactly 1, above 1

Below 1: each doubling takes longer.

What it would feel like: Each new model a little less impressive than the last jump.

Exactly 1: a steady rhythm.

What it would feel like: New abilities on a schedule, like clockwork.

Above 1: each doubling comes sooner.

The map

The spending dial is set from outside the model. It does not model AI earning the money for more research; section 7 covers that extension.

Turn up λ and the line swings down: each unit of AI help is worth more.

Read the map like this. Every point is a possible world, set by two numbers: how hard ideas get (across) and how much AI adds to research (up). The line is every world where the push exactly equals the pull. Below and left of it, the pull wins and growth fades. Above and right of it, the push wins and growth runs away.

Moving the dot up means better AI adds more research effort for each doubling of its ability. Moving it right means past progress helps more. On this illustration ψ would have to rise from about 0.014 to about 0.46. In plain terms: each doubling of AI's ability now brings about 1% more research effort; on the line it would have to bring about 38% more. The red arrow measures the gap from wherever your dot sits: ψ must reach 0.46.

CLAIMED Their appendix gives about 0.35 to 0.45 for what an explosion needs, depending on the version (their post says 0.34 to 0.46). 6. What it implies

A threshold for growth that speeds up by itself. With spending held fixed, the same equation gives a slowdown, steady growth or an explosion, depending on whether λψ + φ is below, at or above 1. Near the line, small changes make enormous differences.

A one-time boost is not an explosion. Below the line, with spending unchanged, a permanent rise in research productivity gives a jump in growth that then fades; above the line it brings the explosion forward.

The before-and-after illustration is far from the line, and the evidence is limited. In an illustration built from Garicano and Imas’s before-and-after comparison (appendix F8, R&D spending, λ = 1), ψ is near zero, but it comes from sixteen model scores at one company, in a period when research was bought, not done by AI. The data say little about what happens once AI does most of the research. The map shows how far ψ would have to move. It cannot say whether it will.

8. Takeaway: three questions, and what would change the answer

You wrote this at the start. Would you change it?

1. Is it a jump, a faster pace, or a pace that keeps rising?

What would move you towards an explosion

  • the time between doublings keeps shrinking across several new models, as in the third panel of section 4.

What would move you away

  • one fast jump, then the old rhythm returns.

2. What makes AI supply more research as it improves, and what limits it?

What would move you towards an explosion

  • the share of research AI leads keeps rising while staff and spending stop rising as fast.

What would move you away

  • AI's share stalls, or labs need ever more computing for each step.

3. Was the research bought, or done by AI?

What would move you towards an explosion

  • progress per dollar stops falling, or starts rising, as AI does more of the work.

What would move you away

  • progress per dollar keeps falling at the old rate, as in Garicano and Imas's data so far.
CLAIMED "leads" means AI completes most of a task end to end while a human supervises.

One last thing. Scroll back up to the loop in section 2. It turns at the speed your settings give, and thirty seconds of watching is ten years in the model. If it slows down, the rising difficulty of finding ideas is winning. If it keeps a steady pace, you are on the line, or spending is doing the work. If it speeds up until it is a blur, you have set up an explosion.
