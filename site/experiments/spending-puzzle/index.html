1. The puzzle

Anthropic spent five times as much on research. Progress only doubled. Has AI research hit a wall?

In November 2026 two sets of numbers about one AI company met on X, and a quick conclusion followed. This page works through that conclusion slowly. It looks at the numbers four ways, and each one teaches something about how economists think.

Here are the numbers.

The money. Anthropic makes the Claude AI models. In the first half of 2025 it spent $1.85 billion on research. In the first half of 2026 it spent $9.74 billion. That is 5.3 times as much.

MEASURED Anthropic's filing with the US markets regulator before selling shares, October 2026. "Research" here covers computers and people together.

The progress. METR is an independent group that tests AI models. It scores each model on one scale and draws a line through the scores. Until October 2025 the line rose 15 points a year. After that it rose 29 points a year. That is 1.9 times as fast.

CLAIMED METR's assessment, 5 November 2026. METR says the true figure could be anywhere from 1.5 to 2.8 times.

The sum. Divide progress by money. In early 2025, each billion dollars came with 4.05 points of progress. In early 2026, each billion came with 1.49. Progress per dollar fell by 63%.

CLAIMED Division of a measured figure by a claimed one, so no surer than METR's line. Michael Wiebe did this sum first.

So: five times the money, twice the speed, and each dollar buying far less. It looks like a field running out of easy wins.

One thing you should know before you go on. Much of the work on this page was done by Claude, and Anthropic makes Claude. Read it with that in mind, and check our sums. The code is open.

2. Compare with last year

What Krier and Wiebe did

Séb Krier writes about AI and policy. On 7 November someone asked him what would convince him that AI is now improving itself. In his answer he pointed at the two numbers above: "a >5x jump in spend for a ~1.9x faster benchmark slope". His reading: "we're already seeing diminishing returns".

MEASURED Krier's post on X, 7 November 2026. "Diminishing returns" means each extra dollar adds less than the one before.

Michael Wiebe, an economist, then did the division and drew the chart: "progress per R&D dollar fell by 63%, even while progress per year doubled."

Two days later Tyler Cowen linked the exchange on his blog under the title "Seb Krier, Ricardian?" David Ricardo was one of the economists who noticed, two hundred years ago, that if you keep adding workers to the same field, each new worker adds less grain.

Their arithmetic is right. We checked it against the filing. Wiebe's chart also carried a warning: "a fall in points per dollar does not establish that AI failed to improve research productivity."

That warning is the subject of the rest of this page.

First question: what would progress per dollar have been without the speedup?

A number means little alone. So ask what progress per dollar would have done if the speedup had never happened.

What if the pace had not changed?

Showing pace doubled.

Spending rose 5.3 times and progress rose 1.9 times. Each dollar bought 63% less progress.

Why?

Look at what the switch shows. With no speedup, progress per dollar falls 81%. With the speedup, it falls 63%. So most of the fall comes from the extra spending, and the speedup made it smaller. The question that matters is whether that spending would have happened anyway, and what it bought.

OUR GUESS Whether the spending would have happened anyway is the question section 3 takes up.

Second question: what does the measure actually measure?

One fact about METR's figures changes how to read the chart. METR did not report how much progress happened in each quarter. It took the scores from before October 2025 and drew the best straight line through them, then did the same for the scores after. It reported only how steep each line was: 15 points a year, then 29. So within each period, the progress figure is the same every month. It was set that way when the line was drawn.

Think of a mango tree. I tell you it grew 30 centimetres a year for five years, then 60 centimetres a year after that. That is all I tell you. Now you ask: how much did it grow per bucket of water each month?

You can do the division. But my straight lines have already fixed the growth at the same amount every month. So your answer goes up and down only with the number of buckets. In a month with twice the water, "growth per bucket" halves. That tells you about the watering. It tells you nothing new about the tree.

Progress per dollar works the same way here. Inside each straight line, it moves only when spending moves. Double the spending and it halves. Spending rose fast, so it fell fast.

ESTABLISHED This is arithmetic. If progress per month is constant, progress per dollar is a constant divided by dollars.

There is a deeper problem, and it would remain even if I had measured the tree every month. Water is not the only thing that changes. Sunshine, rain, soil, wind and the season change too, and you cannot hold them still while you count buckets. You may not even know the full list. So "growth per bucket" mixes the water's effect with everything else that happened that month. AI research is the same: money, staff, cheaper computing, better tools and new ideas all moved at once. Sections 4 and 5 come back to this.

ESTABLISHED Economists call this the problem of holding other things constant. It is why one ratio rarely settles a question of cause.

For now, there is one narrower thing the lines can show: the moment they bend. So the question becomes: what was spending doing at the bend?

3. Compare with where spending was already heading

A grocery bill

A family's grocery bill has doubled every year for three years. This year it doubled again. Did something change in that house this year?

No. Doubling is what that bill does. To find a change, you compare this year's bill with where the old pattern was heading. If the pattern said "double" and the bill tripled, something changed. If the bill doubled, nothing did.

Economists call the old pattern carried forward the trend. Comparing with the trend is how they separate "what happened" from "what would have happened anyway".

ESTABLISHED The standard way to judge an event when things were already moving.

Anthropic's bill

Anthropic's research spending was not sitting still before the speedup. The filings report it over stretches of different length: whole years, half-years and nine months. To compare like with like, the table shows each stretch as an average per quarter, that is, per three months.

Now look at how fast the bill was growing, and notice that the speed itself kept changing. From 2023 to 2024, spending rose about five times. From 2024 to the first half of 2025, it was rising about twice a year. Then in mid-2025 it took off: from the first half of the year to July to September, spending per quarter nearly doubled in four and a half months.

So the old pattern was itself shifting just before the bend. That makes "the trend" hard to pin down.

But which trend?

There is no single right way to carry the old pattern forward. None of the people in this story used a trend: Krier and Wiebe compared with last year, and Garicano and Imas, in section 5, fitted a model. The six below are ours, the obvious ways a careful person might carry the past forward. Each can be defended.

Trends 1, 2 and 3 use only figures from before the bend, and they disagree wildly. Measured against them, spending in early 2026 was anywhere from 0.45 to 2.75 times what the old pattern predicted. Trends 4, 5 and 6 use some later figures, which muddies them.

So the honest answer to "was spending unusual at the bend?" is: it depends on which past you treat as normal. Take the year and a half before the bend as normal, as trend 2 does, and spending raced ahead of its trend about one quarter before progress sped up. That fits Krier's reading: more money first, then more progress. Take only the few months just before the bend, as trend 3 does, and spending looks ordinary.

OUR GUESS Which trend is right is a judgement. A careful reader could defend any of the first three.

Why are these so different?

Take trends 1 and 2. Both are built only from figures before the bend, yet one says spending fell far behind and the other says it raced ahead. Here is where their two numbers come from.

Each number is actual spending in early 2026 divided by what the trend predicted. Above 1, spending ran ahead of the old pattern. Below 1, it fell behind.

Trend 1 copies the pace from 2023 to 2024, when spending rose about 5.4 times in a year. Carried forward to early 2026, that pace predicts about $10.8 billion a quarter. Anthropic actually spent $4.87 billion a quarter. Actual divided by predicted is 0.45. By this yardstick, spending fell well short.

Trend 2 copies the pace from 2024 to early 2025, when spending rose about 1.9 times a year. Carried forward, it predicts about $1.77 billion a quarter. Actual divided by predicted is 2.75. By this yardstick, spending raced ahead.

Same spending, two yardsticks, opposite verdicts. There are two reasons they differ so much.

First, Anthropic's spending growth kept changing. It rose about five times from 2023 to 2024, then about twice a year into early 2025, then surged in mid-2025. Each trend copies a different stretch of that history.

Second, growth compounds. Trend 1's rise of about five times a year, kept up for nearly two years, multiplies spending about 19 times. Trend 2's rise of about 1.9 times a year, for one year, multiplies it by 1.9. A gap between two yearly rates, run over different lengths of time, becomes a huge gap between two forecasts.

Think of measuring a child's height. Measure during a growth spurt, carry that rate forward, and you forecast a giant. Measure during a quiet year and you forecast someone short. Both forecasts use real measurements. Neither tells you the adult's height.

So a better question than "which trend do I like?" is "did this trend predict the figures just before the bend?" The test line under each trend in the panel answers that. No trend passes cleanly.

ESTABLISHED Compounding: a rate applied to a growing amount. Small differences in the rate grow into large differences over time.

The honest conclusion is that the past gives no steady trend to compare with. That, more than any single number, is why the money-versus-AI question is so hard to answer.

Either way, "five times the money" is the wrong yardstick. The question is how much more money went in than would have gone in anyway, and on these figures nobody can say, not even whether it was more or less than usual.

4. Why the public numbers cannot split money from AI

Suppose you could settle on a trend. You still could not say how much of the speedup came from money and how much from AI. Astra, an AI model from a different company, reviewed our reasoning and showed why with a small example.

One kitchen, many stories

A kitchen's budget doubles, and it turns out three times as many meals. How?

Story one: better cooks. The kitchen bought twice as much of everything, and the cooks got half as fast again at turning ingredients into meals.

Story two: cheaper vegetables. The cooks are no faster. But prices fell by a third, so the doubled budget bought three times as much of everything.

Story three: a bit of both. Prices fell a little, and the cooks got a little faster. There is not one such mix but a whole range of them, from all better cooks to all cheaper vegetables, and every one fits the same two numbers.

Why?

From outside, with only the budget and the meal count, you cannot tell which of these kitchens you are looking at. You would need the price list, or a look inside the kitchen.

ESTABLISHED Economists call this an identification problem: different causes that leave the same marks in the data. Here, meals equal ingredients times skill, and ingredients equal budget divided by prices. Any mix in which faster cooks and lower prices together add up to one and a half times as much works.

Anthropic's numbers have the same problem. We see what was spent and how fast the score rose. We do not see how the money was split between people, experiments and training new models. We do not see what a unit of computing cost. And AI help is itself bought with money, because it runs on computers the company pays for. So "money" and "AI" are not two separate piles that the speedup can be sorted into.

What the public numbers support is narrow, and it cuts both ways:

  • Falling progress per dollar does not show that AI failed to speed up research.
  • A speedup with spending near some trend does not show that AI caused it.
OUR GUESS This is our view and Astra's. METR, which saw private evidence we cannot see, guesses that about 80% of the faster pace in October 2026 came from AI. That is a statement about a later date than the bend, and we cannot check it.

5. What more data shows: Garicano and Imas

The day before we finished this page, two economists, Luis Garicano and Alex Imas, published a fuller study of the same numbers. They brought two things our sections above lacked: the scores of sixteen Claude models over time, read from METR's chart, and estimates of how many technical staff Anthropic had.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas, "Are we approaching an intelligence explosion?", Silicon Continent, 13 November 2026, and its online appendix. The staff numbers are their estimates, built from Anthropic's published shares of technical staff.

Over a longer window than ours, they find what Wiebe found. Progress per dollar fell about 69%, close to the 63% above. Progress per technical employee fell about 38%. Spending per employee roughly doubled, which they read as more computing per researcher.

Then they did something our sums could not. They took a standard economist's model of research, in which progress depends on how much effort goes in and on how hard the next idea is to find. They fitted it to the six models before the bend. Then they fed in the money and staff that came after, and asked: does more effort alone explain what happened?

Their answer: not fully. In their most generous version, more spending explains about half the speedup. Measured other ways, it explains much less. The rest looks like a one-time jump in how well research works. They suggest coding agents as one possible cause, and say plainly that their figures are model comparisons, not measured effects of AI tools. Their own caveats matter here: the final score still falls inside the margin of error of their effort-only forecast, and in the most generous version the jump is not statistically clear.

Why splitting the speedup is so hard

Three things changed at once: more money, more staff and better tools. We have one company and one bend. To split the speedup between them, you must assume what would have happened without the tools. Garicano and Imas assume research kept working the way it did before the bend, a pattern they estimate from six scores. Change that assumption, and the split changes.

Timing matters as well. If research spending takes a year to show up as progress, their own appendix finds that the fall in progress per dollar shrinks from about 69% to about 10%. Nobody yet knows how long the delay is.

CLAIMED The 10% figure is in their appendix, under "research delay". The post itself leads with the 69%.

What they conclude about the bigger question

  • Ideas are getting harder to find in AI research too, at about the same rate before and after the bend.
  • There is no clear sign yet of AI feeding its own research in a way that grows as AI gets better. That loop is what an explosion would need.
  • They do not rule one out. On their own figures there is about a one-in-eleven chance that the condition for an explosion is already met, and the data say little about what happens once AI does most of the research.
CLAIMED Each point is theirs, from the post and the appendix. We checked their spending sums against the filings. The staff figures are their estimates, and we did not refit their model.

So their conclusion sits near Krier's: progress sped up partly because more went in and partly from a one-time boost whose size and cause are uncertain, and ideas keep getting harder. The per-dollar chart could not show this. It took the extra data.

OUR GUESS Their work neither confirms nor rules out METR's guess about AI's share, which is about a later date.

6. The step and the staircase

There is one more thing the spending numbers can do, and it is the part of Krier's argument that comes out stronger.

Research gets harder as it goes. The easy discoveries are made first. To keep progress steady, a field needs more and more researchers and equipment each year.

ESTABLISHED Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb, "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?", 2020. They found this across industries, from computer chips to medicine.

How much more each year? Our earlier page, The research machine, just assumed a figure: about 1.4 times a year. Anthropic's own spending suggests a much steeper climb. Through 2025 it was raising research spending somewhere between 2.8 and 5.9 times a year. Both figures include a few months from after the speedup. Until the speedup, growth of that kind was buying steady progress. Garicano and Imas, by a different route, find that keeping today's pace needs inputs to grow about four times a year.

CLAIMED Garicano and Imas's figure assumes a one-time step, not a continuing rise, and that doubling research inputs doubles research effort.

Now take the claim from our earlier page: AI has doubled the pace, which on that page's model is like having about 6 times the research workforce. "Research workforce" there means human researchers and AI doing research work, counted together. Suppose you wanted to hold that doubled pace for five years. How big would the workforce have to grow?

Nobody expects a workforce two trillion times the size. So something in the sum has to give. Perhaps the pace will not hold for five years. Perhaps computing for experiments will grow too. Perhaps AI will change how research is done, and the old pattern will break.

OUR GUESS These figures rest on five assumptions held at once. Change any one and they move a great deal. The second and third stops also treat spending as if it were research effort, which section 4 says we cannot be sure of.

What survives is the difference between a step and a staircase. Speeding up once is a step. Staying that fast means climbing a new, steeper step every year. Krier said as much in his first post: a one-off jump of 1.9 times "isn't the same as sustained exponential" growth. On this point the numbers are on his side.

7. What would settle it

These numbers cannot settle the puzzle on their own. Here is what could.

  1. Real scores, model by model. The straight lines hide what happened quarter to quarter. The scores of each model, with the date it was first used inside the company, would show it. Garicano and Imas have made a start, from METR's chart.
  2. The bill, itemised. Spending split into people, experiments, training new models, and running AI for the staff.
  3. How long spending takes to show up. A delay of a few months or a year changes the per-dollar story completely.
  4. Rival explanations written down first. For example: the old trend continued; there was one jump; the pace changed for good; earlier spending is showing up late.
  5. Rules fixed before the next numbers arrive. Decide now what would count as evidence for each explanation. Then nobody can bend the test to fit the answer, us included.
  6. Modesty about cause. Even a model that predicts the next score well does not prove what caused it. That would take a comparison: a company or a team that did not use AI in its research.
OUR GUESS Most of this plan is Astra's, lightly shortened. Anthropic's next quarterly reports will supply some of item 2. The rest depends on what the company and METR choose to publish.

9. Take away

Your guess

You wrote this at the start. Would you change it?

Next time someone tells you that something "per dollar" has fallen, here are four questions to ask:

  1. Compared with what: last year, or where things were already heading?
  2. What does the measure actually count, and what was fixed before anyone divided?
  3. Could two different causes have produced exactly these numbers?
  4. How long does the money take to show up?
